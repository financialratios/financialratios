Budgeting · 3

Emergency fund, debt & saving goals

Sam's budget leaves $580 a month. Now the real question: what should that money do first? Build a cushion, pay off debt, or start investing? This lesson gives you a simple order of priorities — and the maths behind it.

👴 In one sentence First a cushion for bad days, then kill expensive debt, then let your savings grow.

1. The emergency fund: your personal balance sheet cushion

An emergency fund is money kept aside, in cash, only for real surprises: losing your job, a broken boiler, a medical bill. In the balance sheet lesson we said cash is "a cushion for hard times" for a company. It's exactly the same for a household.

💡 Where to keep it Somewhere safe and quick to reach: a separate savings account or deposit you can access within days. Not in shares — the stock market tends to fall exactly when jobs are lost (see the economic cycle). Keeping it in a separate account also makes it less tempting to spend.

2. Debt: know what each one costs you

Not all debt is equal. What matters is the interest rate. Here are Sam's three debts — their minimum payments are the $150 line in the budget:

Sam's debts in $
Store card · 18% a year · min. $30600
Costs about $9 of interest a month (600 × 18% ÷ 12). The smallest balance.
Credit card · 24% a year · min. $602,400
Costs about $48 a month. The most expensive debt — paying the minimum, it would take Sam almost 7 years to clear it.
Student loan · 5% a year · min. $608,000
Costs about $33 a month. The biggest debt, but the cheapest.
Total debt11,000
About $90 of interest every month — money that buys nothing. With only the minimum payments, Sam would pay over $6,000 in interest over 16 years.
💡 Repaying debt is a guaranteed return Every dollar that pays off a 24% credit card "earns" 24% a year, risk-free. No savings account or investment reliably beats that. That's why high-interest debt (roughly anything above 7–8%) comes before investing.

3. Avalanche or snowball?

Once the starter fund is in place, Sam decides to put $400 extra a month toward debt (on top of the $150 of minimums) and the remaining $180 into the emergency fund. Pay the minimum on every debt, then send all the extra to one debt at a time. When it's gone, its payment rolls on to the next. The only question is the order:

🏔️ Avalanche

Highest rate first

Credit card (24%) → store card (18%) → student loan (5%). Mathematically the cheapest.

⛄ Snowball

Smallest balance first

Store card ($600) → credit card → student loan. Quick wins that keep you motivated.

Sam's planFirst debt goneDebt-free inTotal interest

Avalanche + $400/monthMonth 6 (credit card)22 months~$690

The big lesson is in the first row, not the last two: paying extra cuts the interest bill by almost 90%, whichever order you choose. Avalanche saves a little more money; snowball gives an early victory. If motivation is your problem, snowball is fine. If you're disciplined, avalanche.

⚠️ Don't dig a new hole Paying off a card only helps if you stop adding to it. While repaying, pay new purchases by debit card, and keep the emergency fund so a surprise doesn't go straight back on credit.

4. Saving goals: turn wishes into monthly numbers

Once debts are gone, Sam's $550 of debt payments become free. Any goal becomes a simple division:

Monthly saving = Goal amount ÷ Months until you need it
  • Car deposit: $6,000 in 2 years → 6,000 ÷ 24 = $250 a month.
  • Wedding: $9,000 in 18 months → $500 a month.
  • Home deposit: $30,000 in 5 years → $500 a month, before any interest. The savings goal calculator adds the interest for you.

Money you need within ~5 years belongs in safe places (savings accounts, deposits, short-term government bonds). Money for 10+ years away — like retirement — can be invested in diversified funds, where compound interest does the heavy lifting (see funds & risk). And remember inflation: a goal five years away will cost more than today's price.

The order of priorities

  1. Pay every bill and every minimum payment on time. Late fees and a damaged credit score are the most expensive mistakes.
  2. Build a starter emergency fund (around one month of needs, or $1,000).
  3. Take any employer pension match. If your employer adds money when you contribute, that's an instant 50–100% return.
  4. Pay off high-interest debt — credit cards, store cards, overdrafts, payday loans — with avalanche or snowball.
  5. Complete the emergency fund (3–6 months of needs).
  6. Save for medium-term goals and invest for the long term. Low-interest debt like Sam's 5% student loan can usually just be paid as scheduled.
