Sinking funds: no more "surprise" expenses

Christmas comes every December. The car needs a service every year. Your phone will break one day. These costs are not emergencies — you know they are coming — yet they wreck many budgets because they arrive in one big bill. A sinking fund fixes that: you save a small amount every month for one specific, expected cost, so when the bill arrives, the money is already there.

👴 In one sentence Divide each big future cost by the months until you pay it, and put that amount aside every month.

Where the name comes from

The term comes from corporate finance. A company that issues a bond often promises to put money into a sinking fund every year, so it can repay the bond at maturity without a crisis. The debt "sinks" a little every year. Your household does the same thing with gifts, holidays and car repairs.

Without sinking funds, the emergency fund quietly turns into a "Christmas and car service" fund, and it's empty when a real emergency comes.

The formula

Monthly saving = (Cost − Already saved) ÷ Months until you pay

A $600 Christmas budget, with nothing saved and 12 months to go, needs $50 a month. If you start late, with only 8 months to go, it needs $75 a month this year, then $50 a month from next year on. Starting early makes every sinking fund cheaper.

For costs that come every few years, divide by the full period: a $720 phone that lasts 3 years is $720 ÷ 36 = $20 a month.

How to set them up

  1. List the irregular costs of the last 12 months. Your bank statement is the best memory: look for any bill bigger than a normal week of spending.
  2. Add the ones you know are coming: a wedding next summer, a car service, a new laptop for school.
  3. Calculate the monthly amount with the formula above and add each one as a line in your budget.
  4. Keep the money apart. One savings account is enough. Many banks let you create named "pots", "spaces" or sub-accounts for free: one per fund. If yours does not, a simple spreadsheet can track how much of the balance belongs to each fund.
  5. Automate it. A standing order on payday, like the emergency fund.
  6. Spend it without guilt when the bill arrives. That's the whole point.
💡 Where to keep the money Most sinking funds are spent within 1–3 years, so treat them like the emergency fund: a safe savings account, never shares. Money you need in 12 months should not depend on what the stock market does in 12 months.

Common sinking funds

🏠 Home & car

Car service and tyres, car insurance paid yearly, road tax, home repairs, a new washing machine, furniture.

🎁 Life & family

Gifts, holidays, weddings, school supplies, children's activities, a pet's vet bills.

💻 Things that wear out

Phone, laptop, glasses, winter clothes, a bike. Divide the price by how many years it usually lasts.

🧾 Yearly bills

Property tax, annual subscriptions, professional fees, accountant, bank card fees.

✏️ Check yourself: Maria's car insurance of $840 is due in 7 months. She already has $140 put aside for it. How much should she save each month, and what should she save after this payment?
Show the answer

(840 − 140) ÷ 7 = $100 a month until the bill. After that, the next payment is 12 months away, so 840 ÷ 12 = $70 a month. Starting on time is cheaper: a fund that is always 12 months ahead costs $70 a month, not $100.
