Lesson 5 · Valuation

What is a company worth?

The price is what you pay for a share; the value is what the business is worth to you. There are two families of tools: multiples (quick comparisons) and the DCF (a full model of future cash).

👴 In one sentence A business is worth all the cash it will ever give its owners — and a dollar far in the future is worth less than a dollar today.

Imagine the founders of Rat Bakery offer to sell you shares at $90 per share. With 10,000 shares, the whole bakery is priced at $900,000. This is its market capitalisation (market cap) = price × number of shares.

⚠️ A low P/E is not automatically "cheap" A P/E of 12.5 can be a bargain for a growing company or expensive for one whose profits are about to fall. Multiples only make sense when compared with the company's own history, with similar companies, and together with growth. That's why our analysis pages show each company's multiples over 10 years rather than a verdict.

Part 2 — The discounted cash flow (DCF), step by step

A DCF estimates value from first principles: forecast the free cash flow, then convert future dollars into today's dollars.

Step 1 — Why future money is worth less

$100 today can be invested at, say, 10% and become $110 next year. So $110 next year is worth $100 today. Going backwards like this is called discounting, and the 10% is the discount rate — the yearly return you demand for taking the risk.

Present value = Future cash ÷ (1 + discount rate)years → 110 ÷ 1.101 = 100

Step 2 — Choose your assumptions

  • Starting free cash flow: $58,000 (from lesson 3).
  • Growth for the next 5 years: 5% a year. (Our analysis pages start from each company's own 10-year history; you can change it.)
  • Discount rate: 10%.
  • Growth forever after year 5 ("terminal growth"): 2% — about the long-run growth of the economy. It must be lower than the discount rate.

Step 4 — Everything after year 5: the terminal value

The bakery won't close after 5 years. The value of all later years is estimated with one formula (the "Gordon growth" formula):

Terminal value = FCFyear 5 × (1 + g) ÷ (discount − g) = 74,024 × 1.02 ÷ 0.08 = 943,810
Value today = 943,810 ÷ 1.611 = 586,032

The same bakery is "worth" anywhere from $65 to $100 per share depending on reasonable assumptions. A DCF doesn't give the answer; it shows you what you must believe for a price to make sense. ✏️ Check yourself: why does a higher discount rate give a lower value?
Show the answer

Because you're demanding a higher return. To get more return from the same future cash, you must pay less for it today. Riskier businesses therefore get higher discount rates and lower values.
