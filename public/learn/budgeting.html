Your money plan · Step 2

Step 2: Income & expenses — where does your money go?

A budget is simply a plan for your money: how much comes in each month, where it goes, and what is left. Companies publish an income statement every year; a budget is your own personal income statement — written in advance, so you decide instead of wondering.

👴 In one sentence Write down what comes in, what goes out, and make sure the second number is smaller than the first.

Step 2a: Calculate your income

In step 1, Sam — our made-up 28-year-old with a full-time job — opened an emergency account using a rough estimate of their bills. Now it's time for the full picture, starting at the top. Sam's salary is $3,800 a month before tax, but after income tax and social contributions, $3,000 actually lands in their bank account. That is the number that matters.

💡 Gross vs net pay Gross pay is the salary in your contract. Net pay (or take-home pay) is what you receive after tax. Always budget with net pay — you can't spend money the tax office keeps. It's the same idea as revenue vs net income in a company.

List every source of money that reaches your account, and turn each into a monthly figure:

  • Salary — net, as it arrives. If you get 13 or 14 salaries a year, or an annual bonus, don't count them as monthly income: use them for goals or the emergency fund.
  • Side income — freelance work, tutoring, selling things online. Use a cautious average of the last 6–12 months.
  • Benefits and allowances — child benefit, housing support, meal vouchers.
  • Irregular income (freelance, commission, tips) — budget on your lowest recent month, and treat anything above it as a bonus for savings.

Step 2b: Calculate your expenses

Download the last one to three months of bank and card statements and sort every payment into a category. Then add a monthly share of irregular costs (gifts, car service, annual subscriptions ÷ 12). The result is your real budget.

Fixed vs variable costs

You met this idea in the income statement lesson: rent is a fixed cost for the bakery and for Sam. Fixed costs are paid whatever happens, so they are what makes a budget fragile when income falls. Variable costs can be adjusted month by month.

Fixed costs

45%

Rent, utilities, insurance, loan payments. Cut them rarely, but when you do (a cheaper flat, a better phone plan) you save every month, forever.

Variable costs

36%

Groceries, eating out, fun. Easy to change this month — and just as easy to let creep up without noticing.

How to build your own budget in 4 steps

  1. Find your real take-home pay. Look at what arrived in your bank account in each of the last three months. If it varies (freelance, bonuses, tips), use the lowest month.
  2. Track what you actually spend. Download one to three months of bank and card statements and put every payment in a category. Most people are surprised by at least one line.
  3. Don't forget irregular costs. Car repairs, birthdays, annual subscriptions, the dentist. Add them up for a year and divide by 12: that's a monthly line too.
  4. Write the plan and compare every month. Budget vs actual — exactly what company managers do. Adjust the plan, not your honesty.
⚠️ The "lifestyle creep" trap When income rises, spending quietly rises with it: a nicer flat, a newer phone, more takeaways. A raise only improves your life if part of it goes to the bottom line. A simple rule: save at least half of every pay rise.

Income → $3,300. Spending → 2,420 + 250 = $2,670. Left to save → $630, so the savings rate is 630 ÷ 3,300 = 19.1% — slightly lower than before, despite the raise. That's lifestyle creep in one line. Had Sam kept the old flat, savings would be $880, a rate of 26.7%.
