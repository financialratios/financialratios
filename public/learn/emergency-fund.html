Step 1: Set up your emergency fund

An emergency fund is money kept aside, in cash, only for real surprises: losing your job, a broken boiler, a medical bill. It comes first in the plan because it protects every other step: without it, the first surprise goes on a credit card or forces you to sell investments at the worst moment.

👴 In one sentence Before you invest a single dollar, put a few months of living costs somewhere safe, separate and easy to reach.

Why a company keeps cash — and why you should too

In the balance sheet lesson we called cash "a cushion for hard times". Companies with no cash and a bad year must borrow at high rates or sell assets cheaply. A household is no different: the emergency fund is the cash line on your personal balance sheet.

1. Open a separate account today

Don't wait until you have a full budget — opening the account takes ten minutes and is the first action of the whole plan.

  • Separate from your everyday account. Money you can see on your debit card gets spent. A different account (ideally named "Emergency") adds a small, useful barrier.
  • Safe. A savings account or short-term deposit at a regulated bank. Deposits are protected by government guarantee schemes up to a limit — for example $250,000 per bank per depositor through the FDIC in the US, and €100,000 per bank per depositor in the European Union.
  • Quick to reach. You should get the money within a few days without penalties. Avoid long fixed-term deposits that lock it up.
  • Earning some interest. Compare savings rates — online banks often pay more. But safety and access beat the last 0.5%.
⚠️ Not in shares, not in crypto The stock market tends to fall exactly when people lose their jobs — in a recession (see the economic cycle). An emergency fund invested in shares could be down 30% on the very day you need it.

2. Start with a starter fund

Sam has no savings yet. Building months of expenses takes time, so the plan starts small: a starter fund of about $1,000 (or one month of essential costs, whichever is smaller). That covers most car repairs, a new laptop or a dentist bill — the surprises that usually end up on a credit card.

For a first estimate, Sam opens last month's bank statement and adds up only the bills that must be paid: rent, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance, phone and minimum loan payments. Total: about $1,850 a month. (Step 2 checks this number properly.)

4. Fill it automatically

Set up a standing order: on payday, a fixed amount moves from your current account to the emergency account. Sam starts with the whole $580 until the $1,000 starter fund is reached, then — after expensive debt is cleared in step 4 — goes back to filling it to $5,550. The savings goal calculator shows how long any target takes.

5. Rules for using it

✅ Emergencies

Job loss, urgent medical or dental care, essential car or home repairs, an unplanned trip for a family emergency.

❌ Not emergencies

Holidays, sales, a new phone, Christmas (it happens every year — budget for it!), or "a great investment opportunity".

If you use it, that's what it's for — no guilt. Then make refilling it the top priority in your budget until it's back to target.

✏️ Check yourself: Maria is a freelance designer. Her essential costs are $2,000 a month and she can save $500 a month. What should her full target be, and how long will it take to reach?
Show the answer

With irregular income, aim for at least 6 months: 6 × 2,000 = $12,000. At $500 a month that's 24 months — two years. That's normal! She should reach the $1,000 starter fund in 2 months, then keep going. Any unusually good month (a big project) can top it up faster.
