Step 3: Pick a budget method — 50/30/20 and friends

Knowing where your money goes is step one. Step two is choosing a system that tells each dollar what to do. There is no single best method — the best one is the one you will still be using in six months. Here are the four most popular, tested on Sam's budget from the step 2.

👴 In one sentence Pick simple rules for needs, wants and savings — and make saving happen first, automatically.

1. The 50/30/20 rule

Popularised by US senator Elizabeth Warren and her daughter Amelia Warren Tyagi in their 2005 book All Your Worth. Split take-home pay into three buckets:

Needs

50%

Things you must pay to live and work: housing, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance, minimum debt payments.

Wants

30%

Nice, but optional: eating out, hobbies, holidays, the newest phone.

Savings & debt

20%

Emergency fund, extra debt repayments, investing, retirement.

💡 Use the rule as a compass, not a law With high rent, 50% for needs may be impossible; with a low cost of living, 50% may be generous (then save more than 20%). What matters is the savings bucket: protect it first, and let needs and wants share what's left.

Note the grey area: clothes can be a need (a winter coat) or a want (the third pair of sneakers). Be honest with yourself — that's the whole method.

2. Zero-based budgeting

Every dollar of income gets a job before the month begins, until income minus everything you assigned equals zero. Zero doesn't mean you spend it all: savings are a job too.

Income − spending − saving − debt repayment = 0

For Sam: 3,000 − 2,420 (all spending) − 580 (savings) = 0 ✅. If Sam gets an unexpected $200 refund, it doesn't float around the current account until it disappears — Sam decides immediately: say $150 to the emergency fund, $50 to fun.

Big companies do the same thing with zero-based budgeting: instead of "last year's budget plus 3%", every cost has to be justified from scratch each year.

3. The envelope method

The old-fashioned version: withdraw cash on payday and put it into labelled envelopes — "groceries $350", "eating out $200". When an envelope is empty, that spending stops until next month. Today most banking apps offer the digital version: separate "pots" or "spaces" inside your account.

It works best for variable costs, where overspending happens. It's not needed for rent or insurance, which are paid automatically anyway.

4. Pay yourself first

Most people save "whatever is left at the end of the month" — which is usually nothing. Flip the order: on payday, an automatic transfer moves the savings to a separate account first, and you live on the rest.

💡 Why automation wins Every month you decide "should I save this month?" is a month you can say no. An automatic transfer makes saving the default, so willpower is only needed once — when you set it up.

Methods combine well. A popular mix: pay yourself first for savings, pots for groceries and fun, and a 50/30/20 check once a year.

Habits that make any budget work

  • Review once a month — 20 minutes, same day each month. Compare plan vs actual and adjust.
  • Wait 48 hours before any unplanned purchase over a set amount (say $100). Many "must-haves" don't survive two days.
  • Budget for irregular costs (gifts, car service, annual fees) as monthly amounts, so they are never a surprise.
  • Leave a small "anything" line. A budget with no slack breaks at the first unexpected expense — and then gets abandoned.
